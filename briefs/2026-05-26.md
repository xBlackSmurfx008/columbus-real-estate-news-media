For years, the shuttered Big Lots campus on Dublin-Granville Road has sat as the most visible sign of what a major corporate bankruptcy does to a suburban neighborhood. Twenty-four acres of empty offices and surface lots on a busy northeast Columbus arterial, bought at distressed-sale pricing by OhioHealth in 2025 for $36 million.

Two weeks ago, Columbus City Council cleared the next significant hurdle: a May 11 vote to rezone the property for hospital and associated medical uses. OhioHealth hasn't announced what it's building yet. But the regulatory path is now open.

Here's what the vote means, what comes next, and why northeast Columbus operators are paying close attention.

## A Ghost Campus Gets a Second Life

Big Lots — once a Fortune 500 discount retailer headquartered in Columbus — filed for bankruptcy in September 2024. The company closed stores, wound down operations, and left behind its corporate campus at 4860–4900 E. Dublin-Granville Road: a 24-acre suburban office park that, overnight, became the largest single vacancy on that stretch of road.

OhioHealth, the Columbus-based not-for-profit hospital system, moved quickly. The health system acquired the campus in 2025 for $36 million — a price that reflected the distressed circumstances of the seller. What exactly OhioHealth planned to do with it wasn't announced at the time of the purchase.

The May 11 rezoning vote answers part of that question. The site is now permitted for a hospital, medical offices, outpatient services, and associated accessory uses.

## What the May 11 Council Vote Actually Changed

Before the vote, the property's zoning was calibrated for conventional office and corporate campus use — the kind of development Big Lots built it for. A hospital or large outpatient facility couldn't legally go up under that classification without significant variance requests or a full rezoning.

Columbus City Council's May 11 ordinance reclassified the parcels at 4860–4900 E. Dublin-Granville Road to allow hospital and related accessory uses. Specifically, the new zoning enables medical offices, outpatient services, and the repurposing of existing development "with hospital and associated accessory uses."

The practical effect: OhioHealth can now submit detailed site plans, run traffic and stormwater studies, and apply for building permits — the full permitting sequence that precedes any construction. None of those steps can happen without the underlying zoning being right, and as of May 11, it is.

## OhioHealth's $36M Acquisition in Context

The $36 million purchase price for 24 acres in northeast Columbus requires some context. At roughly $1.5 million per acre, OhioHealth paid significantly below what a comparable suburban Columbus land parcel would cost in a functioning market. The Big Lots bankruptcy accelerated the transaction and likely compressed the price.

For a health system that routinely commits $100 million or more to hospital construction and campus development, acquiring 24 acres of vacant suburban land with existing structures — at what amounts to a distressed discount — is a low-risk way to control a large site before making the capital commitment to build.

What OhioHealth actually builds could vary substantially. The rezoning permits the full range of medical uses: a full-service hospital, an outpatient surgery center, medical office buildings, or some combination. The health system hasn't disclosed a preferred program.

OhioHealth has not yet determined how the property will ultimately be used. As of the time of the council vote, the health system is expected to advance design and technical studies — the kind of work that typically precedes a public announcement of scope and timeline.

## The Permitting Road Ahead

The rezoning is a necessary step, not the last step. Before any construction begins, OhioHealth will need to clear a full permitting sequence: detailed site plans, building permits, and technical reviews focused on traffic, stormwater management, and parking — all standard for a large suburban medical campus.

The traffic component alone can take months. Dublin-Granville Road is a major northeast Columbus arterial with significant existing traffic load. A hospital or large medical facility would materially change the trip generation profile of the site, and ODOT and city engineering typically require traffic impact studies and mitigation plans as conditions of approval.

There is no publicly announced construction timeline.

## What It Means for the Dublin-Granville Corridor

The Dublin-Granville corridor in northeast Columbus is one of the city's primary suburban commercial belts — office parks, retail strip centers, medical buildings, and residential subdivisions running east from I-270 toward the New Albany border.

A hospital or large medical campus on the former Big Lots site would be the largest institutional anchor investment on that corridor in years. The effects on nearby operators are significant:

**Medical office demand.** Hospitals generate satellite demand for medical offices, specialty practices, and outpatient services in surrounding areas. Operators holding existing medical office inventory near the site — or considering new development — have a potentially material demand driver coming.

**Retail and service demand.** Large medical campuses generate steady foot traffic and employment. Nearby retail and food-service operators could see meaningful traffic lift once the campus is operational.

**Residential demand.** Hospital employment creates workforce housing demand. Northeast Columbus neighborhoods — Gahanna, Westerville, and New Albany edge communities — could see incremental demand from OhioHealth employees if the campus is built at significant scale.

**Land pricing.** The $36M transaction established a reference price for distressed large-site acquisition on the corridor. If OhioHealth's plans are announced at scale, comparable sites nearby will reprice.

Columbus's northeast suburbs lost a large employer when Big Lots departed. What OhioHealth does with the campus next is the biggest single redevelopment story on this corridor in years — and Columbus City Council's May 11 vote moved it meaningfully forward.
