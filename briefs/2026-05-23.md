## Columbus Rents Drop 3.5% — What the Numbers Miss

Columbus has one of the largest rent declines in the United States this May. But whether that trend affects your tenants — or your portfolio — depends entirely on which Columbus you're invested in.

A widely-cited May 2026 Redfin report, aggregated by platforms including Rent.com, RentCafe, and Zumper, shows the median asking rent in Columbus falling **3.5 percent year-over-year** — the third-largest decline of any major U.S. metro, trailing only Minneapolis and Austin. Columbus Underground flagged the number with a pointed question: "Are They Really?"

The answer, as it usually is in real estate, is: it depends.

---

## The Headline Number in Context

The 3.5% decline is not made up. The average asking rent in Columbus sits at approximately **$1,425 per month** as of mid-May 2026, per Rent.com — down roughly $30 from the same period in May 2025. Some aggregators report a figure closer to $1,341, reflecting different weighting of unit types and neighborhoods.

For context, Columbus rents remain 27% below the national average — about $524 less per month than what renters pay nationally. That positioning has made Columbus a consistent draw for households relocating from higher-cost metros, and it's one reason the National Association of REALTORS named Columbus a [top-10 housing hot spot for 2026](https://www.ohiorealtors.org/blog/2282/ohio-housing-markets-land-in-the-national-spotlight-for-2026/).

Sitting alongside Minneapolis and Austin in a rent-decline category is unusual territory for Columbus. Those markets represent two very different stories — Minneapolis has a well-documented construction surplus, while Austin saw years of speculative multifamily development followed by a sharp correction. Columbus's presence in that tier is partly a function of its own 2023–2025 construction surge, and partly a function of how the data is measured.

---

## What the Data Actually Measures

The rent-decline figure comes from commercial listing platforms that aggregate data from large, professionally managed apartment communities. As Columbus Underground correctly notes, these platforms primarily list "newer, larger apartment complexes."

In practice, that means the data:

**Includes:** Newly built and recently renovated Class A and Class B apartments in managed communities, typically 50+ units, listed on platforms like Rent.com and Redfin — properties in Short North, the Arena District, Italian Village, German Village, Grandview Heights, Dublin, New Albany, Polaris, and similar submarkets.

**Excludes:** Income-restricted affordable housing units and properties developed with Low-Income Housing Tax Credits. Smaller landlords and portfolio owners who list units informally. Single-family rentals, duplexes, and accessory dwelling units. Most of the South Side, Linden, Franklinton's older housing stock, and other neighborhoods where Columbus's workforce rental market actually operates.

The 3.5% decline is measuring saturation and pricing pressure in the luxury and upper-moderate multifamily segment — not the Columbus rental market as a whole.

---

## Two Columbus Rental Markets

Columbus operators and investors are watching two separate rental markets play out simultaneously, and the dynamics are running in opposite directions.

**Market 1: New-construction institutional multifamily.** This is the market the Redfin data captures. Columbus saw a significant surge in luxury apartment construction from 2023 through 2025, with hundreds of new units delivering across downtown, the Short North, German Village, Italian Village, and near suburbs. That supply wave arrived as interest rates tightened, compressing the renter pool that could afford premium pricing. The result was measurable: Greater Columbus multifamily vacancy hit a record 10.6% in Q4 2025. While the pace of new construction starts has slowed, the pipeline of already-permitted projects continued delivering through early 2026. Operators in this segment are competing on amenities and offering concessions — one to two months free rent — to close leases.

*[Internal link: "[Edwards Companies Shelves $600M Capitol Square Renaissance](../2026-05-03-edwards-companies-capitol-square-renaissance-shelved.md)" — May 3, 2026 — for context on what high-end downtown delivery conditions look like right now]*

**Market 2: Workforce and affordable rentals.** Asking rents in neighborhoods serving Columbus's lower- and middle-income renters have not followed the luxury market downward. One-bedroom apartments in Forest Park East average $749 per month. In Old North Columbus: $750. In Franklinton: $799. These price points, serving households earning $30,000–$55,000 per year, reflect a market with structurally insufficient supply. The city's pipeline of income-restricted affordable units is growing — NRP Group's Dering Family Apartments (245 units along South High Street) is expected to break ground late 2026, and a 58-unit senior housing project at 42 W. Jenkins Ave. has city approvals and is pursuing Low-Income Housing Tax Credits — but the pace is measured against a waitlist for subsidized units that runs years long.

---

## What This Means for Columbus Operators

**If you operate Class A multifamily:** The 3.5% decline is operative in your market. Expect continued concession pressure through 2026 as the 2024–2025 construction pipeline finishes absorbing. The structural story remains positive: the rate of new construction starts has slowed, and by 2027–2028 the supply overhang should be largely resolved. Columbus's demand fundamentals — population growth, job creation in healthcare, technology, and logistics, continued in-migration from higher-cost metros — haven't changed. This is a cycle, not a structural break.

**If you operate Class B/C multifamily or small residential portfolios:** Your market is not the market described by the headline. Workforce housing in Columbus is structurally undersupplied. Rent.com and Redfin data say nothing meaningful about what's happening in Linden, the South Side's single-family rental market, or older Franklinton stock. The competitive pressure Class A operators are feeling is not the same pressure you're facing. Cost management — deferred maintenance, energy efficiency, property tax appeals — matters regardless of rent trajectory, but you are not in a concessions environment.

**For development underwriting:** Tighter lending conditions and more conservative cap-rate assumptions are the reality across the market. But the rent-decline data applies most directly to luxury new construction in well-supplied submarkets. A workforce apartment project in Whitehall, a value-add acquisition in Hilliard, or a tax-credit project on the South Side each carries a different rent-trend story. Apply the data at the submarket and asset-class level — not as a market-wide assumption.

---

## The Policy Layer: Zone In and the Affordability Gap

Columbus City Council recently approved Phase 2 of the Zone In initiative — the city's most significant zoning overhaul in 70 years. Phase 2 covers 43% of city parcels (approximately 66,000 properties), introducing no parking minimums, looser height and density rules, and six new mixed-use districts. The intent is to unlock faster, more flexible development, particularly for housing.

Over the next three to five years, Zone In's Phase 2 implementation should make it meaningfully easier to build missing-middle housing — duplexes, townhomes, small apartment buildings — in the middle ring of the city. That is the supply type most needed to bridge the gap between the luxury multifamily market softening and the affordable housing market that remains structurally undersupplied.

*[Internal link: "[Columbus Zone In Phase 2 Approved: What It Means for Developers]()" — forthcoming coverage]*

The affordability gap in Columbus is not a 2026 story. It's a structural challenge that will outlast any single market cycle. What the rent-decline data should prompt Columbus operators to ask is not "is the market soft?" — it depends on which market — but "which Columbus am I actually serving, and what does that mean for my strategy through 2027?"

---

## The Bottom Line

To answer Columbus Underground's question directly: yes, rents are down in Columbus — in the market you can see on Rent.com. In the Class A and upper-moderate multifamily segment, the 3.5% headline is accurate and reflects real concession pressure from a construction surplus that is still working through the system.

In the Columbus rental market that houses the majority of the city's renters — workforce households, seniors on fixed incomes, families in older housing stock — the data tells you very little. That market remains supply-constrained, and the affordable pipeline being built to address it will take years to deliver.

Columbus operators who understand the difference are positioned to make better decisions. Those who apply the luxury headline to the full market are working with an incomplete map.
