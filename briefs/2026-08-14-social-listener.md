### 1. Zone In Meets Neighborhood Pushback — A City-Wide Rift Emerges
**Volume:** Loud | **Sentiment:** Frustrated (neighborhood groups) / Confident (city/developers) | **Frame:** Threat (to narrative that Columbus zoning reform is broadly welcomed) + Content idea

Columbus's "Zone In" rewrite — the city's first top-to-bottom zoning overhaul in 70 years — is producing an explicit political rift between city leadership and neighborhood organizations. A WOSU Public Media report from December 2025 headlined "Rift appears between city leaders and neighborhoods over Columbus' growth plan" documented that multiple area commissions are pushing back on the speed and scope of changes, particularly along high-frequency transit corridors where upzoning to allow denser, taller buildings is being advanced without site-by-site variance hearings (the tool historically used by neighbors to delay or reshape projects they opposed).

The Columbus CEO outlet confirmed in an August 4, 2026 piece that civic and business leaders have formally united to "move the needle on housing" — a coalition framing that signals developers and city hall are aligning on Zone In's pace while neighborhood voices feel sidelined.

Zone In Phase 2 will cover an additional 43% of the city. Draft building types, heights, transition rules, and corridor standards are being developed through 2026 with another round of public comment. Area commissions are nominally included in that process but critics argue the outcome is already determined.

Sentiment is split along predictable lines — neighborhood associations worry about loss of community veto power over adjacent development; urbanist and affordability advocates argue that same veto power is what has kept Columbus's housing supply constrained for decades.

> "Columbus City Council reminded residents that zoning changes are coming early next year that will usher in more dense urban developments, with code changes such as allowing taller buildings, fewer required parking spaces, and reducing the need for site-by-site community negotiations for 'variances.'" — *Yahoo News / Columbus Dispatch, confirmed in search index*

> "There are mixed feelings among Clintonville commissioners around the best ways to achieve more housing options, more affordability, and more density." — *City of Columbus Zone In public engagement summary, confirmed in search index*

**Content gap:** "Who wins and who loses when Columbus's zoning code finally changes?" — A neighborhood-by-neighborhood explainer of which corridors get upzoned, what's likely to actually get built, and who controls the process now. No local outlet has mapped this clearly for a general audience. High search value; relevant to investors, residents, and policy watchers alike.

### 2. Rental Registry — Landlords Are Gearing Up to Fight
**Volume:** Steady | **Sentiment:** Angry (landlords) / Supportive (tenant advocates) | **Frame:** Threat (if you cover landlord/investor audience) + Content idea (policy explainer)

Columbus City Council passed a citywide rental registry ordinance on April 20, 2026, creating Columbus City Code Chapter 4515. Every residential rental property within city limits must now register annually with the Department of Building and Zoning Services, providing unit counts, parcel IDs, ownership information, and a 24/7 emergency contact. The registry is paired with new property inspection requirements: up to 10% of units eligible for inspection; properties with violations can face mandatory third-party technical inspections at owner cost; a $250/year fee applies to units in conditional status.

The landlord community's reaction has been hostile. Multiple property owner groups threatened legal challenges before the ordinance passed. Key objections: (1) added cost and administrative burden on small landlords; (2) concern that a centralized registry enables selective enforcement targeted at certain neighborhoods or property types; (3) argument that large institutional landlords with compliance staff benefit relative to mom-and-pop owners.

Tenant advocates and the city counter that the registry gives Columbus the data it needs to hold "faraway landlords" accountable — absentee owners who are often unreachable when code violations arise.

This is a live, contested ordinance with compliance deadlines coming. The conversation is active in both landlord forums and tenant rights circles.

Direct quotes from named landlords not available.*

**Content gap:** "Columbus's rental registry is now law. What do landlords actually have to do, and by when?" — A step-by-step compliance guide written for the small-portfolio landlord audience. Also: "What tenants can do with the new registry" — a companion piece for renters. Neither piece has been written for a non-specialist Columbus audience.

### 3. $500M Affordable Housing Bond — Community Demands Transparency on Where the Money Goes
**Volume:** Steady | **Sentiment:** Cautiously supportive / suspicious (transparency concerns) | **Frame:** Content idea + watchdog opportunity

Columbus voters approved a $500 million affordable housing bond in November 2025 — the city's third housing bond but by far the largest. Mayor Ginther laid out the framework in February 2026: ~$150M for construction and preservation of affordable rental units; ~$125M for transitional housing, shelter capital, home improvements for low-income owners, and permanent supportive housing; remainder to be allocated through a competitive application process.

The community engagement process that preceded the framework was notable in scale: the Department of Development contacted 183 housing stakeholders representing 137 organizations over six months. But the follow-up demand is for real-time transparency on where funds are being awarded, not just process consultation.

Columbus City Council President Shannon Hardin advanced legislation to make the application and award process more transparent, and the Columbus Department of Development is building an online dashboard where residents can see funded projects.

An editorial — published by bluewaterhealthyliving.com, August 2026 — argues the $500M is insufficient at current construction costs: "Columbus can't do it alone. $500M for affordable housing isn't enough." It frames the bond as a necessary but inadequate response to a structural housing shortage that requires state and federal intervention.

**Content gap:** "Columbus's $500M housing bond: what's been funded so far, and what neighborhoods are getting it?" — A mid-year accountability report tracing awarded dollars to specific projects and neighborhoods. This is exactly the kind of watchdog journalism that builds loyal local readership. The city dashboard (if/when live) would make this story possible from public data alone.

### 4. Intel Delay — But Housing Demand in New Albany Is Already Here
**Volume:** Light-to-Steady | **Sentiment:** Uncertain / speculative | **Frame:** Opportunity (housing market story) + threat (for buyers banking on Intel-adjacent price appreciation)

Intel's $28B semiconductor campus in New Albany / Licking County has been pushed from its original 2025 production target to a 2030–2031 start. The delay is real — Intel Corporation formally announced it in 2023/2024 due to subsidy negotiations and market conditions — and construction continues, with roughly 800 workers on site and 1.6M+ work hours logged as of early 2025.

Despite the delay, Intel's presence is already reshaping the housing market northeast of Columbus. Local real estate agents on record say they are fielding calls from current Intel employees as well as investors from California, Oregon, and Arizona seeking homes in the vicinity. Available housing near the site is described as "scarce."

The Licking County / New Albany spillover effect — where buyers who can't afford inner-ring Columbus neighborhoods look further east — is compressing values in markets that were historically much more affordable. This is a story that hasn't been told locally from the buyer-displacement angle.

Infrastructure is following the money: roundabout construction at SR 605/Walnut, SR 37/County Line, and Fancher Road/County Line is funded ($4-5M per intersection) and expected to complete in 2026.

**Content gap:** "Intel says 2030. But Columbus real estate agents say the buyers are already here." — A ground-level story on who is buying in New Albany, Pataskala, and Johnstown right now and what the Intel delay means for the investment thesis. Strong investor-audience angle; no local outlet has written this clearly in 2026.

### 5. Franklinton's Two Speeds — Development Accelerating, Displacement Fears Growing
**Volume:** Steady (ongoing narrative; no single new event) | **Sentiment:** Anxious / conflicted | **Frame:** Threat (gentrification displacing long-term residents and artists) + Content idea

Franklinton — Columbus's oldest neighborhood, west of downtown — is experiencing what longtime residents describe as two parallel realities. The "Bottoms Up" gentrification narrative (well documented by Ideastream/WOSU, Columbus Underground, and academic research) describes rapid post-flood-wall development pressure squeezing out artists, small businesses, and low-income households who anchored the neighborhood through decades of disinvestment.

New City Homes is completing the first of 50 planned new single-family homes in Franklinton, with most slated to finish by end of 2025/early 2026. A hospitality group described in search results has chosen Franklinton for its first out-of-market restaurant expansion. These are signals of the neighborhood reaching a tipping point.

A 2026 academic study published in the Journal of the American Planning Association (Taylor & Francis) — "Gentrification-Induced Displacement, Destination Neighborhoods, and Destination Houses for Low-Income Households in Columbus, Ohio" — documents that displaced Columbus households typically land in neighborhoods with somewhat less poverty but higher housing costs than they could previously afford, and that displacement in Columbus does not primarily push residents to suburbs (contrary to some other cities' patterns). This is peer-reviewed data that local journalism has not yet made accessible to a general Columbus audience.

> "We need more units and we know that will help to bring rents down, make things more affordable." — *Mayor Andrew Ginther, on Franklinton housing plans, confirmed in search-indexed NBC4 coverage*

This single political quote reflects the city's supply-side stance, but it sits uneasily against the documented experience of artists and small businesses who say they are already being pushed out by development pressure and rising rents before new supply arrives.

**Content gap:** "Where do Franklinton residents actually go when they're displaced?" — A data-driven story based on the 2026 JAPA study, translated for a local audience. Could include interviews with Franklinton Development Association or local affordable housing advocates. Strong human-interest angle with credible academic sourcing.

### 6. Rising Short North Rents — Luxury Creep Reaching New Benchmarks
**Volume:** Light (no single viral moment; cumulative signal) | **Sentiment:** Negative (renter frustration) / Positive (developer confidence) | **Frame:** Content idea + threat (to narrative of Columbus affordability)

*Note: Covered partially in Aug 11–12 briefs. Flagged here because a new data point has emerged.*

Average Short North rents climbed 6.49% year-over-year as of August 2026 — the neighborhood's highest annual increase in this cycle. Parkside on Pearl, the neighborhood's newest luxury tower, is now leasing units from $2,495 (880 SF, one bedroom) to $8,000/month (1,840 SF top-floor three-bedroom). The $8,000 figure is the highest-publicized single-unit rent in the Columbus market and has appeared in multiple aggregator sites, generating organic sharing and negative reaction from renters who treat it as a symbol of broader affordability failure.

The Short North average rent of ~$1,668/month is still below the Parkside on Pearl entry point, but the gap between what most people see as "Short North rent" and what the neighborhood's newest buildings actually charge is widening — and is likely to show up more in social conversation as the Parkside leases up.

**Content gap:** "What $1,668 vs. $8,000/month tells us about the Short North rental market in 2026" — A rent-tier explainer that breaks down the difference between the legacy stock and the new-construction market, and what it means for who can actually afford to live in the Short North going forward. Strong social sharing potential (both for those who find the prices aspirational and those who find them outrageous).
