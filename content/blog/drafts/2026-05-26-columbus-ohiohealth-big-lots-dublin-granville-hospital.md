For years, the shuttered Big Lots campus on Dublin-Granville Road has been the most visible sign of what a major corporate bankruptcy does to a suburban neighborhood. Twenty-four acres of empty offices and surface lots on a busy northeast Columbus arterial, sitting idle after the retailer's collapse — until OhioHealth acquired it in 2025 for $36 million.

Two weeks ago, Columbus City Council cleared the next significant hurdle: a May 11 vote to rezone the property for hospital and associated medical uses. OhioHealth hasn't announced what it's building. But the regulatory path is now open.

Here's what the vote means, what comes next, and why northeast Columbus operators are paying close attention.

## A Ghost Campus Gets a Second Life

Big Lots — once a Fortune 500 discount retailer headquartered in Columbus — filed for bankruptcy in September 2024. The company closed stores, wound down operations, and left behind its corporate campus at 4860–4900 E. Dublin-Granville Road: a 24-acre suburban office park that became the largest single vacancy on that stretch of road, essentially overnight.

OhioHealth, the Columbus-based not-for-profit hospital system, moved quickly. The health system acquired the campus from the bankruptcy estate in 2025 for $36 million — a price that reflects the distressed circumstances of the seller. What OhioHealth planned to build wasn't announced at the time.

The May 11 rezoning vote answers part of that question. The site is now cleared for a hospital, medical offices, outpatient services, and associated accessory uses.

## What the May 11 Council Vote Actually Changed

Before the vote, the property was zoned for conventional office and corporate campus use — calibrated for exactly the kind of development Big Lots had built there. A hospital or large outpatient facility couldn't legally be built on the site without significant variance requests or a full rezoning.

Columbus City Council's May 11 ordinance reclassified the parcels at 4860–4900 E. Dublin-Granville Road to allow hospital and related accessory uses. Specifically, the new zoning permits medical offices, outpatient services, and the repurposing of existing buildings "with hospital and associated accessory uses."

The practical effect: OhioHealth can now submit detailed site plans, commission traffic and stormwater studies, and move through the building permit process — the full sequence that precedes any construction. None of that can happen without the underlying zoning being right, and as of May 11, it is.

## OhioHealth's $36M Acquisition in Context

The $36 million purchase price for 24 acres in northeast Columbus requires some framing. At roughly $1.5 million per acre, OhioHealth paid below what a comparable suburban Columbus land parcel would cost in a functioning market. The Big Lots bankruptcy compressed the transaction timeline and likely compressed the price with it.

For a health system that routinely commits $100 million or more to hospital construction and campus development, acquiring 24 acres of suburban land with existing structures — at a distressed discount — is a low-risk way to control a large site before committing capital to build.

What OhioHealth actually builds could vary substantially. The rezoning now permits the full range of medical uses: a full-service hospital, an outpatient surgery center, medical office buildings, or some combination. The health system has not disclosed a preferred program or scope.

As of the council vote, OhioHealth is expected to advance design and technical studies — the work that typically precedes a public announcement of scope and timeline.

## The Permitting Road Ahead

The rezoning is a necessary step, not the last one. Before construction begins, OhioHealth will need to clear a full permitting sequence: detailed site plans, building permits, and technical reviews covering traffic, stormwater management, and parking. These are standard requirements for a large suburban medical campus, and they take time.

Traffic review alone can stretch for months. Dublin-Granville Road is a major northeast Columbus arterial, and a hospital or large medical facility would materially change the trip generation profile of the site. ODOT and city engineering typically require impact studies and mitigation plans before final permits are issued.

There is no publicly announced construction timeline.

## What It Means for the Dublin-Granville Corridor

The Dublin-Granville corridor in northeast Columbus is one of the city's main suburban commercial belts — office parks, retail strip centers, medical buildings, and residential subdivisions running east from I-270 toward New Albany. The Big Lots campus sits near the middle of it.

A hospital or large medical campus at this location would be the largest institutional anchor investment on the corridor in years. The effects on nearby operators are real:

**Medical office demand.** Hospitals pull satellite demand for medical offices, specialty practices, and outpatient services in the surrounding area. Operators holding existing medical office inventory nearby — or considering new development — have a potentially significant demand driver on the horizon.

**Retail and service demand.** Large medical campuses generate consistent foot traffic and employment. Nearby retail and food-service operators could see a material traffic lift once the campus is operational.

**Workforce housing demand.** Hospital employment at this scale creates demand for workforce housing. Northeast Columbus neighborhoods — Gahanna, Westerville, and communities on the New Albany edge — could see incremental rental demand if OhioHealth builds significant staff capacity here.

**Land pricing.** The $36M transaction established a reference price for distressed large-site acquisition on the corridor. If OhioHealth's development plans are announced at scale, comparable sites nearby will reprice accordingly.
