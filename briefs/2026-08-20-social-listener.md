**1. New Columbus Zoning Code Takes Effect August 28 — Community Split** — Loud, Mixed
*Frame: Content opportunity + threat (negative sentiment surfaces at top per policy)*

The biggest Columbus real estate conversation right now is the new zoning code, officially adopted by City Council in July after 70+ years without a comprehensive overhaul. It goes live **August 28, 2026** — eight days from now. The code creates six mixed-use districts along key corridors, eliminates parking minimums, and loosens height and density restrictions — unlocking a theoretical capacity of up to 88,000 new housing units (versus only 6,000 under the old code).

Community reaction is sharply split. Neighborhood groups and residents are voicing concerns about traffic, parking loss, and developer access. YIMBY-aligned advocates are celebrating. Critics — notably in coverage from the Columbus Dispatch — accuse the city of doing developers' bidding by stripping neighborhood veto power over variances.

**Verbatim (resident comment, NBC4 / 10TV coverage of zoning hearings, via search summary):**
> "The main concerns that residents are voicing include the impact on traffic and parking." — composite of community input from local news coverage, August 2026 *(NBC4/10TV — direct page fetch blocked, sourced via search summary)*

**Verbatim (commercial real estate perspective, NAI Ohio Equities analysis):**

**Verbatim (opposition frame, Columbus Dispatch via PressReader, 2026):**

Ginther and city council members — by removing ways that neighborhood groups and residents can interfere with their building projects." — Columbus Dispatch editorial/reporting summary

**Volume signal:** Loud — multiple distinct outlets, community organizations, and public meeting participants representing divergent perspectives. Not a single-source amplification.

**Negative-sentiment surface (priority per policy):** The developer-capture accusation is substantive and sourced. Multiple distinct voices (resident groups, Dispatch editorial) have made it. CRE News should not soften or omit it.

**2. Apartment Supply Wave Is Softening Rents — But Not in Affordable Tiers** — Steady, Mixed
*Frame: Content idea — data explainer that the community is implicitly asking for*

Columbus is in the middle of a substantial multifamily supply wave. Q1 2026 data: 1,600 units delivered in the quarter, 11,000 units under construction, average asking rent $1,400/unit, annual rent growth just +0.7% — effectively flat. Short North specifically saw rents *fall* 3.67% YoY (average now ~$1,857/mo). The pipeline is keeping vacancy elevated and operators are competing on concessions.

The social tension: this supply wave is overwhelmingly Class A / market-rate. The conversation in community spaces reflects confusion — people are hearing "more housing is coming" but still cannot find affordable units. Franklinton average is $1,529/mo; Clintonville $1,300/mo; Columbus median overall $1,366/mo. None of these numbers are affordable for households at 60–80% AMI without subsidy.

**Verbatim (Matthews Real Estate Q1 2026 market report, via search summary):**
> "Annual rent growth was modest at 0.7%, indicating that operators had limited pricing power amid elevated competition from new supply." — Matthews Columbus Multifamily Q1 2026

**Verbatim (community housing frame, City of Columbus via search):**
> "Columbus City Council approved $10 million for the Central Ohio Community Land Trust to boost construction of below-market homes for local families earning up to 120% of area median income." — City of Columbus news summary, 2026

**Volume signal:** Steady — consistent across multiple data providers (Matthews, Redfin, RentCafe, Zumper, Zillow) with converging numbers. Reddit searches didn't return direct posts but the data alignment is strong.

**3. Franklinton and Near East Side Displacement — Undercovered, Ongoing** — Steady, Negative/Frustrated
*Frame: Threat (to community) + content opportunity (underserved audience)*

Multiple sources document a persistent gentrification/displacement pattern in Franklinton and the Near East Side. Academic research published in 2025 (Journal of the American Planning Association) confirms a "strong connection between gentrification and displacement" in Columbus — displaced households move to better poverty neighborhoods but worse housing situations. Arts spaces have already left Franklinton due to developer pressure. Community land trust advocates are trying to hold the line.

This conversation continues in background mode in social spaces — not a daily trending topic, but a chronic concern across neighborhood Facebook groups, local journalism comments, and advocacy organizations. The new zoning code's Aug 28 implementation is reigniting it: critics worry that parking-min removal and density bonuses will accelerate gentrification in exactly these neighborhoods.

**Verbatim (Ideastream/WOSU reporting on Franklinton, 2024 — still live/relevant):**
> "Two decades after the completion of the flood wall, Franklinton is seeing explosive development, and now gentrification threatens to displace the neighborhood's families. Some artist and art spaces have already left the neighborhood because of pressure from developers or rising rent prices." — Ideastream Public Media, Feb 2024

**Verbatim (academic research summary, 2025):**
> "Results indicate a strong connection between gentrification and displacement, and displaced households typically move to better neighborhoods in terms of poverty, but to worse housing situations." — Journal of the American Planning Association, 2025 *(source: tandfonline.com)*

**Volume signal:** Steady — not spiking, but multiple distinct voices (residents, researchers, advocacy orgs, journalists) over a sustained period. Not manufactured.

**4. Mercy on Main — NFL Alum's Affordable Housing Proposal in Olde Towne East** — Light, Positive
*Frame: Content opportunity — human interest + LIHTC / affordable financing story*

Kingsley + Co. (founded by ex-NFL player Chinedum Ndukwe, a Notre Dame/Bengals/Raiders alum) is proposing "Mercy on Main" — a 65-unit, five-story affordable housing development at 907–917 E. Main St. in Olde Towne East. The $22.8M project received a 9% Low Income Housing Tax Credit award from Ohio Housing Finance Agency (one of 25 statewide). Community reception from the Near East Area Commission and Olde Towne East Neighborhood Association has been described as positive. The project mix: 8 studios, 29 one-BRs, 18 two-BRs, 10 three-BRs.

**5. Intel Fab + Regional Housing Pressure East of Columbus** — Light, Curious/Concerned
*Frame: Content opportunity — underreported regional story*

Intel's New Albany fab complex has logged 9.4M+ work hours through 2025 and is on track for initial production. The question "where are we putting everybody?" — originally raised in 2022 — remains unresolved, particularly for lower-wage construction workers. National coverage (Fortune, NPR, NBC News) captured this in 2022; local follow-through on the actual housing impact in 2025–26 is sparse.
