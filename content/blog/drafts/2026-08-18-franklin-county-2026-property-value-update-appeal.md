Franklin County mailed updated 2026 property assessments to all county homeowners on June 9, and the average residential value rose about 9 percent from the 2023 full reappraisal. If you own real estate in Franklin County, the informal review window with the Auditor's Office is open now and runs only through September — the easiest, lowest-cost step for contesting a value you believe is too high.

## What the 2026 Triennial Update Is

Ohio law requires county auditors to update property values every three years between full reappraisals, a process called a triennial update. The 2026 update is Franklin County's midpoint check between the last full reappraisal, conducted in 2023, and the next one scheduled for 2029.

For most Franklin County homeowners, the June 9 mailing was the first formal notice of a new appraised value. The Franklin County Auditor's Office also posted values online and launched a dedicated resource page — "Know Your Home Value" — that walks through timelines, a printable guide, and review options.

Important to understand upfront: the new 2026 values affect your 2027 tax bill, not your current one. Your 2026 tax payments are still calculated on the 2023 reappraisal values.

## Why Values Went Up — and What 9% Means

The 9% average increase reflects how Franklin County home prices moved between the 2023 reappraisal and now, per the Franklin County Auditor's Office. It is an average, meaning some areas and property types moved more or less than that figure.

Here is the arithmetic on a typical case. If the Auditor assessed your Columbus home at $275,000 in 2023, a 9% update would bring the new appraised value to roughly $299,750. In Ohio, residential properties are taxed on 35% of their appraised value — what the state calls "taxable value." So your taxable value would rise from approximately $96,250 to roughly $104,913, an increase of about $8,663. The actual tax-dollar impact depends on your specific millage rate, which varies by city, township, and school district — and may be partly offset by Ohio's homestead exemption if you qualify.

For Columbus-area investors managing multiple properties, the effect compounds across every parcel. A landlord with five rental properties each assessed around $250,000 is looking at a combined assessed-value increase of about $112,500 in taxable base — worth reviewing carefully if any of those values look out of line with current market evidence.

## The Informal Review: Free, Low-Stakes, and Closes in September

The informal review period runs from July through September 2026. It is not an adversarial hearing — it is a scheduled meeting with the Auditor's Office where you present evidence that your new appraised value does not reflect your property's actual market value.

What constitutes evidence:
- A recent appraisal or comparable sales data showing similar properties sold below your assessed value
- Documentation of significant physical problems with the property not reflected in the assessment
- A recent arms-length purchase price lower than the assessed value

The informal process costs nothing. If the Auditor's Office agrees your value should be adjusted, they change it before it is finalized. If you disagree with the outcome, you move to the next step.

To request an informal review session, contact the Franklin County Auditor's Office directly at the "Know Your Home Value" resource page or by phone.

## If the Informal Review Doesn't Resolve It

Homeowners and property owners who remain unsatisfied after the informal review can file a formal complaint with the Franklin County Board of Revision by March 31, 2027.

The Board of Revision is a three-member panel — typically the county auditor, treasurer, and a county commissioner or their representatives — that holds quasi-judicial hearings. You present evidence; the board rules. If the board does not rule in your favor, you can appeal further to the Ohio Board of Tax Appeals.

The formal complaint process is more involved than the informal review. Most property owners — and particularly residential homeowners with a straightforward market-value disagreement — will get the best result from the informal review. Save the formal complaint for cases where the disagreement is significant and you have solid evidence.

## Who Should Act Now

Not every property owner needs to contest their value. If your new appraised value is in line with what you could sell the property for today, paying the associated taxes is simply the cost of owning a property that has appreciated.

Property owners with the strongest case for review include:

- **Owners of properties with known issues** — deferred maintenance, foundation problems, outdated systems — that are not reflected in the automated valuation
- **Landlords and investors** who purchased a property in 2024 or 2025 at a price below the new assessed value (a recent purchase price is strong evidence)
- **Owners of atypical properties** — irregular lot sizes, non-conforming structures, easements — where mass-appraisal methods tend to produce the least accurate values
- **Anyone whose assessed value exceeds what comparable nearby properties are actually selling for** right now, which can be checked against Columbus REALTORS data or Redfin sale prices in your neighborhood

## Context: Where Columbus-Area Values Stand

The central Ohio housing market hit a median sale price of approximately $335,000 across the region in mid-2026, according to Columbus REALTORS data, with inventory growing about 7% year-over-year. That means homes are still selling — just a bit slower and with slightly more buyer leverage than in 2023 or 2024.

For Franklin County's triennial update, the relevant question is not what homes are worth today, but what they were worth on the assessment date the Auditor used. If the Columbus market has softened from its 2024 peak in your specific neighborhood, a well-documented comparable-sales argument could support a downward adjustment.

The informal review is the place to make that argument before September ends.
