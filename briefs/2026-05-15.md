Ohio lawmakers formed a bipartisan Joint Data Center Committee this week to investigate the land-use, energy, and environmental impacts of the state's data center boom — and the story begins and ends in suburban Columbus.

The committee's first meeting is May 27–28, 2026. For Columbus-area real estate operators with industrial, agricultural, or suburban assets in the I-270 exurban ring, the proceedings are worth watching closely.

## What the Committee Is and Who's On It

The Ohio Joint Data Center Committee was announced May 13, 2026, as a joint initiative of the Ohio House and Senate. Co-chairs are Sen. Brian Chavez (R-Marietta) and Rep. Adam Holmes (R-Nashport). Additional members include Rep. Thad Claggett (R-Newark), Rep. Heidi Workman (R-Rootstown), Rep. Chris Glassburn (D-North Olmsted), Sen. Bill Reineke (R-Tiffin), Sen. Shane Wilkin (R-Hillsboro), and Sen. Willis Blackshear (D-Dayton).

The panel is a bicameral, bipartisan body. Its agenda covers five areas:

- **Energy demand** — hyperscale AI facilities are among the largest industrial electricity consumers; Ohio's regional grid operator MISO has flagged increasing load pressure
- **Farmland conversion** — ag-to-industrial land sales in Licking County have accelerated, prompting concern from rural communities
- **Noise pollution** — cooling equipment and backup generators at large facilities generate 24/7 sound in areas that were once farmland or low-density residential
- **Water use and disposal** — cooling tower consumption at hyperscale facilities is significant and growing
- **National security** — concentration of critical AI and cloud infrastructure in a single geography raises resilience questions at the federal level

The Ohio Farm Bureau, which represents the state's agricultural landowners and has watched the farmland conversion issue closely, welcomed the committee's formation on May 14. "There are many unanswered questions," lawmakers said in their announcement.

## Why Columbus Is Ground Zero

Ohio's data center buildout began — and remains centered — in New Albany. Since 2010, technology companies have opened 40 data centers at the New Albany International Business Park, roughly 18 miles northeast of downtown Columbus. As of 2026, 28 more are on the drawing board or under construction at that location alone. Across the broader Columbus metro, 133 data centers are now operating — the highest concentration in the state, and one of the densest clusters in the country.

What made New Albany attractive also made it a template. The formula: proximity to fiber infrastructure, large-lot greenfield sites converted from farmland, and a 15-year 100% property tax abatement that eliminates carrying costs during lease-up. Dublin, Hilliard, and Marysville have all replicated versions of the playbook. A $1 billion data center was recently approved for Weaver Road in Marysville under the same abatement structure [needs source: developer name behind Anadonna LLC registration].

Columbus is now projected to surpass New York City to become the second-largest data center hub in the Great Lakes region — after Chicago — as Ohio's pipeline of planned facilities comes online, according to a January 2026 WOSU report.

## The Constitutional Amendment Shadow

The committee's formation doesn't exist in a policy vacuum. A grassroots campaign has been building since late 2025 to put a constitutional amendment on Ohio's November 2026 ballot that would ban construction of data centers with aggregate power demand exceeding 25 megawatts. The Ohio Ballot Board unanimously approved the petition drive on April 3, 2026, clearing organizers to begin signature collection.

To reach the ballot, petitioners need signatures from 5% of registered voters across at least 44 of Ohio's 88 counties — a high bar, but one that is achievable given the breadth of concern across rural, exurban, and suburban communities. "Farmland is getting bought out," said Andrew Gula, a member of the petition committee.

If the amendment passes in November 2026, it would effectively halt most hyperscale data center construction in Ohio.

St. Albans Township in western Licking County — already home to some of New Albany's nearest agricultural neighbors — has effectively banned data centers by removing "data processing services" from its local zoning regulations. That local-level action is part of a broader pattern of township and county-level zoning resistance spreading through the Columbus exurbs.

For CRE operators and developers, the constitutional amendment track is the higher-stakes watch item. The committee can study and recommend; a constitutional amendment, if passed, codifies restrictions that are extremely difficult to undo.

## What This Means for Columbus-Area CRE Operators

**Industrial and suburban land holders:** The 15-year 100% tax abatement that has been the primary lure for data center tenants is exactly the fiscal mechanism the committee will scrutinize. If the legislature conditions or eliminates abatements following the committee's work, cap rates for data-center-adjacent industrial land in New Albany, Marysville, and the northwest suburban ring could shift.

**Ag-to-industrial conversion plays:** Farmland adjacent to existing data center campuses in Licking County has been among the most liquid conversion targets in the Columbus metro. If constitutional restrictions advance — or if township-level zoning bans proliferate — the developable land pipeline thins and remaining parcels command a scarcity premium. Timing matters.

**Utility and grid capacity underwriting:** MISO load forecasts tied to AI workloads are already affecting electricity infrastructure planning in central Ohio. Industrial tenants — data centers and conventional manufacturers alike — increasingly require power capacity confirmation before signing leases. Investors underwriting industrial assets in the Columbus tech corridor should include utility capacity due diligence as a standard deal item.

**Brokers and general market operators:** The New Albany tech corridor has been a primary driver of industrial land absorption in the Columbus metro for more than a decade. If Ohio's regulatory posture toward data centers tightens materially by 2027, that demand pressure either redirects to other Columbus industrial submarkets or compresses — affecting vacancy and rent trends across the broader industrial market.

This is not a story about whether data centers are good or bad for Columbus. It is a story about regulatory uncertainty arriving at a moment when billions of dollars in facility investment and land conversion are in motion. Uncertainty tends to pause deals until the picture clarifies.

## Watching the May 27 Hearings

The committee's inaugural meeting is scheduled for May 27–28, 2026. No agenda or witness list has been published as of this writing. Legislative study committees of this type typically invite state agency officials from the Ohio Development Services Agency, the Ohio Environmental Protection Agency, and the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio, alongside industry representatives — likely including Meta, Amazon, and Google or their trade associations — and community advocates.

Columbus-area developers, the Building Industry Association of Central Ohio, and the Columbus Partnership should assess whether their interests are represented in testimony before May 27. [needs source: confirmation of any Columbus industry presence at the committee]
