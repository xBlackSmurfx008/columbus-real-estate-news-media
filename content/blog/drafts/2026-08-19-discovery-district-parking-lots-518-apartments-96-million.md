# Discovery District Parking Lots to Yield 518 Apartments in $96M Deal

**Category:** Development  
**Tags:** columbus-ohio, central-ohio-real-estate, downtown-columbus, development, multifamily, discovery-district  
**Area:** downtown-columbus | **Topic:** development  
**Date:** 2026-08-19 | **Author:** CREN Staff

Columbus City Council approved an economic development agreement June 29 clearing the way for Pizzuti Companies and Encova Insurance to convert surface parking lots near Oak and Washington into roughly 518 apartments and 15,000 square feet of retail — a $96 million phased project backed by city-funded street upgrades and a planned 575-space public parking garage.

## How a parking portfolio becomes a neighborhood

Encova Insurance, the Columbus-based mutual insurer, has spent years acquiring surface lots in the Discovery District — the stretch of downtown east of Fourth Street, between COSI and the Nationwide Arena corridor. Through its real estate affiliate, Washington Oak Realty, the company holds 47 parcels clustered near its headquarters at 471 E. Broad St.

The firm eventually brought in Pizzuti Companies, a developer already operating on the district's western edge with three completed phases of the Library Park apartments and a fourth in planning. On June 29, council formalized the partnership with an economic development agreement — the public instrument that commits the city to specific infrastructure roles in exchange for the private development moving forward, as first reported by Columbus Business First.

## What the city is paying for

The agreement authorizes Columbus's Department of Development and Department of Public Service to seek $1.65 million from the 2026 capital improvement budget. Those dollars cover design work for Oak Street improvements between Ninth Street and Lester Drive, along with water and sewer upgrades in the project area.

Construction of those improvements is slated for future budget cycles. Separately, the city intends to build a 575-space public parking garage for the district — a move designed to replace parking that would disappear as surface lots are converted to housing, so nearby institutions and businesses don't lose access.

## 518 units and 15,000 square feet of retail

The phased development would deliver roughly 518 residential units and up to 15,000 square feet of ground-floor commercial space. That retail footprint works out to about 29 square feet per apartment — consistent with neighborhood-serving uses like a café, pharmacy, or dry cleaner rather than a destination draw.

The $96 million total-investment figure covers all phases combined. Per-phase breakdowns, building heights, and construction timelines have not been publicly disclosed. Pizzuti declined to comment on design, affordable housing plans, or timeline at the time of the original reporting.

## Precedent from Library Park — and what it means here

Pizzuti built its Library Park development in phases along the Discovery District's western edge over several years — a model that proves phased projects in this corridor can reach completion. Library Park is now a functioning residential neighborhood that has helped activate the stretch of Grant Avenue near the Columbus Metropolitan Library main branch.

The Discovery District's institutional anchors — COSI, the Columbus Museum of Art campus, and Nationwide Children's Hospital to the south — draw hundreds of thousands of visitors annually. But the walk from the Short North or the convention center often cuts through an unusually quiet stretch of surface lots and fencing. Adding 518 housing units would introduce a permanent residential base to support ground-floor retail and extend pedestrian life further east.

## Why the city's involvement matters

Projects of this scope in under-activated parts of downtown frequently stall at the design or financing phase. Cities that commit infrastructure dollars alongside private capital — improving streets, committing to a parking garage — reduce the project risk that causes deals to drag. Columbus has done both here.

The Zone In zoning reform that council has been advancing allows taller, denser buildings along major corridors, and the Discovery District sits adjacent to several of them. That regulatory backdrop makes permitting easier once design is finalized.

Full buildout of 518 units across multiple phases in a part of downtown that has historically been slow to densify could still span many years. But the city's June 29 commitment is a material step — and it put the project on the public record in a way it wasn't before. For anyone tracking [downtown Columbus development](/topics/development), this is the combination of public and private investment worth monitoring closely.
