# Downtown Columbus: Edwards Shelves $600M Mixed-Use Plan

Edwards Companies announced it is shelving the Capitol Square Renaissance — Columbus's largest planned downtown mixed-use development — citing unfavorable market conditions. The project, which would have transformed roughly 10 acres south of East Broad Street with more than 1,000 residential units, nearly 200,000 square feet of office space, nine restaurants, and multiple parking decks, carried a price tag of approximately $600 million.

For Columbus real estate operators and investors, the decision is more than a single developer's choice. It is a marker: the most ambitious downtown Columbus development plan of the current cycle is on hold, and the reason given — too much existing product to absorb — reflects a supply reality that has been building for several years.

---

## What Was the Capitol Square Renaissance?

The Capitol Square Renaissance was a proposal by Columbus-based Edwards Companies to comprehensively redevelop approximately 10 acres of land south of East Broad Street, in the blocks surrounding the Ohio Statehouse on Capitol Square. The plan called for more than 1,000 new residential units, nine restaurants, nearly 200,000 square feet of office space, public green space, and multiple parking decks.

The project received considerable public support. It secured up to $33 million in state tax credits. In December 2025, Columbus City Council approved one component of the project — a 12-story, 218-unit apartment building at 33 South Fourth Street — marking the development's first piece of formal approval.

The Preston Centre, a conversion of the former PNC Tower, was completed as part of the broader Capitol Square corridor effort and stands as the Renaissance project's tangible legacy on the ground today.

The Capitol Square corridor has been one of the most closely watched areas in downtown Columbus. The combination of state government offices, major law firms, and proximity to the central business district has historically made it both high-visibility and difficult to develop — land costs, historic context, and market timing have all factored into previous delays. The Renaissance plan was the most serious attempt yet to move the area decisively forward.

---

## Why Edwards Is Pressing Pause

Edwards Companies CEO Jeff Edwards gave a measured explanation: "While we remain confident in the long-term trajectory of downtown Columbus, today's market conditions indicate that now is not the right time for this project."

That phrase — "market conditions" — does significant work. A local real estate expert, cited by WOSU following the announcement, said developers "realize downtown is saturated with product and they need to pause to let existing product be absorbed." [expert name not confirmed — needs attribution before final publish]

The supply picture in downtown Columbus makes that framing credible.

The Merchant Building — the 32-story mixed-use tower adjacent to North Market, carrying 174 apartments, a 206-room hotel, and 68,000 square feet of office space — is expected to complete by late 2026. [internal link: CRE News coverage of the Merchant Building construction milestone] The Centennial on High, Nationwide Realty Investors' conversion of 280 N. High Street into 148 apartments, is also in the pipeline for 2027. [internal link: CRE News coverage of Centennial on High Downtown Commission approval]

Those two projects alone represent a material near-term addition to downtown supply. Against that backdrop, underwriting a 1,000-unit mixed-use development requiring coordinated financing, city approvals, and phased construction across multiple asset classes is a very different ask from the market of 2022 or 2023.

Downtown office vacancy in the CBD stands at 18.46%, lower than the overall metro rate of 19.18%, and posted its third consecutive quarter of positive absorption in Q4 2025. That is genuinely positive momentum. But large-scale mixed-use financing still requires confidence across all asset classes in a project simultaneously — and achieving that confidence in the current rate environment has proven difficult for developers across Columbus and nationally.

## What's Already Built — and What's Still Standing

The shelving of the Capitol Square Renaissance does not mean nothing has been accomplished.

The Preston Centre — the adaptive reuse of the former PNC Tower within the broader Capitol Square corridor — has already been completed, adding office and mixed-use capacity to the area. It stands as the clearest physical evidence of what the larger Renaissance plan was working toward.

The 12-story apartment building at 33 South Fourth Street represents a more uncertain situation. Columbus City Council approved that component in December 2025. Whether it will move forward independently of the larger Capitol Square Renaissance plan has not been confirmed publicly. ## What This Signals for the Downtown Columbus Market

The Capitol Square Renaissance pause is a signal, not a verdict.

Columbus has been building as aggressively as any peer city. The metro issued 9,548 multifamily units in permits in 2025 — a record, and a 36% year-over-year increase — ranking #9 nationally in multifamily permitting. [internal link: CRE News multifamily permitting coverage] That pipeline is still moving. What has paused is the highest-risk, largest-scale tier of downtown mixed-use development.

For Columbus operators, the distinction matters:

**Downtown multifamily** vacancy is still below 5%, and rents have grown approximately 4.2% year-over-year through Q1 2026. The market has absorbed supply well. But the Merchant Building and Centennial on High will test that absorption in real time over the next 18 months — and a 1,000-unit addition on top of them is a different risk profile.

**Class A suburban office** is telling a different story. The Pointe at Polaris — a 245,437-square-foot fully-leased mixed-use asset in the Polaris corridor — was just acquired by DFW LAND, a Dallas investor, in a transaction brokered by Newmark. [internal link: CRE News Pointe at Polaris deal coverage — scheduled for next week] Out-of-state institutional capital is still moving into Columbus suburban CRE. The flight-to-quality and flight-to-suburban trends that began nationally post-2020 are still playing out here.

**Industrial** remains the tightest segment in the metro. Columbus industrial vacancy ended Q1 2026 at 7.2%, down from 9.7% in 2024, with six consecutive years of rent growth. [internal link: CRE News Columbus industrial market coverage]

The shelving is also a data point for Columbus's relationship with state economic development incentives. $33 million in credits was awarded to a project that is now on hold. That does not mean the credits are lost — their disposition will be determined by OHFA and the Ohio Development Services Agency — but it raises legitimate questions about how incentive structures calibrate for project execution risk at the time of award.

---

## What to Watch Next

Several indicators will signal whether the Capitol Square Renaissance timeline is revisited — and when:

**The 12-story apartment at 33 S. Fourth St.** If Edwards Companies moves this component forward independently, the pause is strategic and partial. If it also stalls, the retreat is more complete.

**Downtown absorption data from the Merchant Building and Centennial on High.** Both projects will lease up in 2026–2027. If they absorb quickly at strong rents, the case for revisiting Capitol Square's residential program in 2027–2028 becomes materially stronger.

**The $33M in state tax credits.** OHFA's and the Development Services Agency's decisions on credit disposition will be the first official indicator of how state government views the project's future.

**Interest rate trajectory.** Edwards Companies didn't name rates specifically, but "market conditions" for a $600 million mixed-use project always includes the cost of capital. A meaningful rate reduction in 2026–2027 could reopen the underwriting math.

Columbus has absorbed large project pauses before and moved forward. The development fundamentals that made Capitol Square a credible $600 million target — central location, strong jobs base, growing downtown residential demand — haven't changed. What has changed is the near-term supply stack and the capital markets patience required to bridge to absorption.

The question isn't whether Columbus downtown can support this kind of development. It's when the math works again. That answer belongs to the market.
