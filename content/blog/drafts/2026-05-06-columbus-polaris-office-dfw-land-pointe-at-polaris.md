# Columbus Polaris Office Sale Signals Suburban CRE Demand

The Columbus office market just told two different stories in the same week — and reading them together matters more than reading either one alone.

On April 29, 2026, Dallas-based DFWLAND acquired the fully leased office and retail components of The Pointe at Polaris in Columbus's Polaris submarket. Newmark brokered the transaction and arranged acquisition financing. The deal covered two Class A office buildings totaling 212,366 square feet and 33,071 square feet of retail — 245,437 square feet in total. Every square foot was occupied at closing.

Three days earlier, Edwards Companies had announced it was shelving its $600 million Capitol Square Renaissance project downtown, citing current economic and market conditions. ([See CRE News coverage of the Edwards decision.](/blog/2026-05-03-edwards-companies-capitol-square-renaissance-shelved))

The pairing isn't a coincidence. It's a map of where Columbus office capital is moving right now.

---

## What Sold — and What Didn't

The transaction was surgical in scope. DFWLAND acquired only the office and retail components of the larger Pointe at Polaris campus. Two residential buildings on the property, together containing 473 luxury apartment units, remained with the seller and were not part of this transaction.

The office component is anchored by a blue-chip tenant roster:

- **Bank of America** — financial services
- **Fiserv** — financial technology
- **Power Home Remodeling** — regional employer
- **Employers Health** — healthcare benefits administration

The retail component — also fully leased — brings a complementary amenity mix to the office buildings:

- Kitchen Social
- Condado Tacos
- Sweetwaters Coffee & Tea
- Club Pilates

The result is a campus where an office worker can grab coffee, get lunch, and take a fitness class without leaving the property. That kind of embedded amenity density is what separates the Polaris submarket's top-tier product from commodity suburban office elsewhere in Franklin County.

## Who Is DFWLAND?

DFWLAND is a Dallas-based commercial real estate investment firm with a portfolio that spans multiple asset classes and markets. CEO Vijay Borra said at the time of closing: "The acquisition of The Pointe at Polaris adds another top-tier investment to our growing portfolio."

Chief Investment Officer Brian Rosen elaborated on the investment rationale: "The Pointe at Polaris encompasses sought-after office and retail tenants in a multi-use environment that includes multifamily and a hotel." That last detail — citing the multifamily and hotel adjacency even though DFWLAND did not buy those components — signals that the mixed-use campus context was part of the investment thesis, not just the office buildings in isolation.

VanTrust Real Estate and partner NP Limited Partnership developed The Pointe at Polaris as a mixed-use campus along Interstate 71, directly across from Polaris Fashion Place — the 1.3 million-square-foot regional shopping mall that has anchored the Polaris corridor's commercial identity for two decades.

## Reading the Columbus Office Split

The juxtaposition of the Polaris deal and the Edwards news is the most instructive data the Columbus office market has produced in 2026.

Downtown Columbus office faces a genuine challenge: new construction requires tax credit support, pre-leasing hurdles are high, and the Edwards decision to pause a project that had already received $33 million in state Transformational Mixed-Use Development credits shows how difficult the downtown pro forma is even with public subsidy. That's not a Columbus-specific problem — it mirrors dynamics playing out in urban cores across the Midwest.

The Polaris market is operating on different fundamentals:

**Occupancy is the admission ticket.** The Pointe at Polaris offices were 100% leased at the time of sale. In today's institutional capital environment, that's not a nice-to-have — it's what separates financeable assets from ones that sit. Investors and their lenders can underwrite a fully occupied building; they cannot easily underwrite the lease-up risk on a new downtown tower.

**National tenants reduce credit risk.** Bank of America and Fiserv are recognizable names with investment-grade credit. Lenders can price debt against those leases. That tenant quality, concentrated in a suburban submarket, is what allowed this deal to get done cleanly.

**Submarket positioning beats vintage alone.** The Pointe at Polaris benefits from its location: I-71 access, proximity to the region's largest retail center, and a surrounding cluster of corporate users who have made Polaris a de facto headquarters neighborhood for Central Ohio financial and healthcare businesses. Location is doing work that building age alone cannot.

**Mixed-use adjacency has option value.** DFWLAND specifically mentioned the multifamily and hotel components as part of why they bought the office buildings — even though they didn't buy those buildings. The mixed-use environment is a hedge: if office occupancy softens in a future cycle, the property is surrounded by compatible uses that support long-term land value.

## What This Means for Columbus Operators

**Polaris is a flight-to-quality destination.** Suburban Columbus office investment is concentrating in Polaris-submarket assets with modern vintage, full occupancy, and amenity density. Older suburban product in outlying Franklin County submarkets without these attributes will face continued headwinds by comparison. The valuation gap between Polaris trophy and commodity suburban office is likely to widen.

**Out-of-state capital is watching Columbus.** DFWLAND is a Texas firm. Non-local institutional buyers acquiring Columbus suburban product signal that the market has passed the credibility threshold for investors who have no pre-existing regional relationship. For Columbus brokers marketing suburban office investment opportunities, that broadens the buyer pool.

**The residential buildings didn't trade with the office.** VanTrust retained the 473-unit apartment component. Watch for whether those buildings eventually come to market separately — or whether the retained residential position becomes the foundation for a longer-hold campus strategy. The two components can be priced and financed differently, and the separation preserves optionality for both parties.

**Cap rate discovery is still incomplete.** No sale price was publicly disclosed. Until a deed recording surfaces through the Franklin County Auditor, the market doesn't have a public data point on where Polaris trophy office is pricing in 2026. When that recording becomes available, it will set reference expectations for other Class A suburban Columbus assets evaluating a sale in the next 12–24 months. ([See LinkUS West Broad BRT delay coverage](/blog/2026-05-05-columbus-west-broad-brt-delay-hilltop-franklinton) for how different the suburban and urban investment timelines have become this spring.)
