### 1. Zone In Phase II — Community Split on Upzoning
**Volume:** Steady | **Sentiment:** Mixed | **Frame:** Content idea + threat (to neighborhood-opposition narrative)

Columbus is deep into the second phase of its "Zone In" zoning reform, which will rezone 66,000+ parcels — 43% of the city. City Council passage is targeted for spring 2026 after community input sessions in early 2026. The civic conversation is actively divided.

Supporters argue the rezoning along transit corridors (especially Cleveland Ave and West Broad) will add needed housing without forcing current residents out. Critics — and they're vocal — say the code overhaul removes neighborhood groups' ability to block developer projects and is designed to benefit major donors to Mayor Ginther and council members. A recurring flashpoint: parking requirements. Concern about sub-1:1 parking ratios in new developments is widespread enough that the city announced a parking study.

> **Representative signal** (Columbus Underground comment thread on zoning proposal responses): "The proposed upzoning along Cleveland Avenue would bring much-needed housing to one of Columbus' most-affordable neighborhoods, right on the CMAX bus route, letting new people join the community without forcing current residents out." — cited in Columbus Underground community coverage, *source: columbusunderground.com* [URL indexed but domain blocked from direct fetch]

> **Opposing signal** (public comment period, via Columbus Underground): Critics say the overhaul is "being done to please developers — major political donors to Mayor Andrew J. Ginther and city council members — by removing ways that neighborhood groups and residents can interfere with their building projects." — paraphrased from public record, *source: columbusunderground.com/we-asked-for-responses-to-the-citys-zoning-proposal-heres-what-we-heard-bw1/*

### 2. The Affordability Paradox — Columbus "Looks Affordable" But Thousands Left Behind
**Volume:** Steady | **Sentiment:** Frustrated / Negative | **Frame:** Content idea + threat (reputation risk for "affordable city" branding)

Average Columbus rent is $1,366/month, up 1.4% YoY — numbers that look manageable in national comparisons. But a NewsBreak investigation framed the hidden crisis clearly: workers earning near minimum wage, seniors on fixed incomes, and cost-burdened families face severe stress the averages mask. The city has a deficit of tens of thousands of affordable units for its lowest-income renters.

Neighborhood variation is stark: Short North, German Village, and the University District run well above average; Columbus's south and east sides are significantly cheaper but often underserved by transit and amenities.

> **Representative signal** (NewsBreak, "Columbus Rent Crisis 2026: The Numbers Look Affordable, But Thousands of Renters Are Still Being Left Behind"): "A city can have lower-than-national-average rents while still experiencing a serious affordability problem." — *source: newsbreak.com/crafting-your-home-355087212/4808651217814-columbus-rent-crisis-2026* [domain blocked from fetch; headline and framing confirmed via search index]

**Content gap:** "Why does Columbus get called affordable when I can't afford it?" — a plain-English piece that walks through the math for a $15/hr worker, a teacher, a nurse, explaining the gap between the citywide average and what these workers actually face.

### 3. The Intel Effect — Northeast Corridor Still Running Hot Despite Delay
**Volume:** Steady | **Sentiment:** Mixed (opportunity for investors, pressure for workers) | **Frame:** Opportunity + content idea

Intel pushed its production start from 2025 to 2030-31. But construction is actively underway — 800+ workers on site, over 1.6 million work hours logged, workforce expected to grow to several thousand by year-end. The "Intel Effect" is reshaping the northeast corridor (New Albany, Johnstown, Pataskala) in real time.

New Albany median home price: ~$625,000. The top Franklin County residential sale in June 2026 was a $3.2M transaction in New Albany. The Columbus metro needs an estimated 14,000–19,000 new homes per year through 2032; it is adding a fraction of that.

Investor conversation is active: per RL Property Management's 2026 market report (search-indexed), Columbus is "one of the strongest single-family rental markets in the Midwest" with occupancy above 95% on well-managed properties. The source-of-income protection ordinance (in effect since 2024, barring landlords from refusing Housing Choice Vouchers) is generating investor discussion — with some landlords noting voucher tenants produce "reliable, government-backed rent."

> **Representative signal** (RL Property Management, 2026 Columbus Market Report): "Columbus skews more toward appreciation than raw cash flow relative to Toledo or Cleveland, but it offers a long runway of structural demand growth backed by one of the largest corporate capital investments in American history." — *source: rlpmg.com/2026-columbus-market-report/* [domain blocked from fetch; language confirmed via search index]

**Content gap:** "Where are Intel construction workers actually living right now?" — a ground-level story on housing for the current 800-person workforce, particularly those commuting from outside the region. No outlet appears to have done this.

### 4. Franklinton Transformation — Opportunity Arrives, Displacement Anxiety Intensifies
**Volume:** Loud | **Sentiment:** Mixed-to-negative among existing residents | **Frame:** Threat + content idea

Franklinton is the convergence point for 2026 Columbus development. Multiple projects landing simultaneously:
- **Gravity Phase 3 ("The Greenhouse"):** 310+ apartments
- **Astor Park:** 261 multi-family units + riverfront parks near Lower.com Field
- **LinkUS BRT West Broad Corridor:** construction timeline now delayed 16 months (see Theme 6)
- **New townhome developments** along West Broad

The structural tension: nearly 70% of Franklinton residents are renters. The Franklinton Development Association has explicitly stated its mission now includes "address[ing] affordability for Franklinton's renters" who "will face displacement brought on by the changing characteristics of the community."

The Central Ohio Community Land Trust is targeting 500 homes by 2026 — land trust acquisition is one of the few tools being used to pre-empt displacement before appreciation fully arrives.

> **Representative signal** (Franklinton Development Association, mission statement): "...will face displacement brought on by the changing characteristics of the community." — *source: franklinton.org / cap4kids.org/columbus/franklinton-development-association/*

**Content gap:** "Who gets to stay in the new Franklinton?" — a profile-based story tracking 2-3 current Franklinton renters as development arrives, following the Central Ohio CLT's land trust strategy alongside market-rate delivery.

### 6. LinkUS BRT Delay — Design Changes Possible on West Broad
**Volume:** Light | **Sentiment:** Frustrated / Concerned | **Frame:** Threat (to TOD timeline) + content idea

COTA's LinkUS project — critical for transit-oriented development along West Broad and East Main corridors — faces a 16-month construction delay after ODOT traffic projections came in double earlier estimates. Design changes to the West Broad corridor are now possible. East Main Street BRT is at 90% design, targeting 100% by year-end.

The delay matters for housing: the West Broad TOD plan was explicitly designed to layer affordable and attainable housing around BRT stations. A 16-month slip compresses the window for proactive land acquisition before station-area appreciation.

> **Representative signal** (COTA Board consultant presentation, via Columbus Underground): "A 16-month delay estimate comes from a consultant presentation to the COTA Board, after [ODOT] issued new projections that showed traffic doubling along the corridor compared to previous estimates." — *source: columbusunderground.com/cota-linkus-construction-delay-changes-to-west-broad-design-possible-bw1/* [URL confirmed; domain blocked from direct fetch]

**Content gap:** "What does the LinkUS delay mean for property values near planned stations?" — both investors and current residents are asking this. No local outlet has written the timeline-to-price-effect explainer.
