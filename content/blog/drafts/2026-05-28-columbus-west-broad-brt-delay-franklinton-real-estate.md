## Columbus West Broad BRT Delay Hits Franklinton Real Estate Plans

The 16-month construction delay on Columbus's West Broad Street bus rapid transit line, confirmed by COTA last week, is more than a transit headline. For developers, investors, and operators who have positioned along the West Broad corridor in Franklinton and the Hilltop, it's a material shift in the infrastructure timeline that underpinned many of those bets.

Here's what we know — and what it means for the market.

---

## What COTA Said

On May 27, 2026, COTA confirmed to WOSU Public Media that the West Broad Street BRT construction timeline is "fluid." The agency is still coordinating with the City of Columbus and the Ohio Department of Transportation to resolve remaining design elements before submitting final details to the Federal Transit Administration for key federal funding approvals.

The 16-month delay flag isn't new. It first surfaced in an April 23 WOSU report, when engineering consulting firm HNTB told COTA's board of trustees that the schedule had slipped approximately 16 months. The root cause: ODOT issued revised traffic volume projections in May 2025 that roughly doubled earlier estimates for the corridor. That forced a reassessment of design assumptions for a critical one-mile section in Franklinton between Princeton Avenue and May Avenue.

COTA has said the dedicated bus-only guideway lanes planned for that Franklinton section will not be eliminated — even as the geometry of the corridor undergoes potential rework.

The original projected construction start was 2026. With a 16-month delay, construction is now unlikely to begin before late 2027 at the earliest, pushing BRT service on West Broad to 2028 or 2029. [needs source: COTA has not publicly confirmed a revised service launch date as of this writing]

---

## The Franklinton Redesign Zone

The one-mile stretch between Princeton Avenue and May Avenue in Franklinton is the most consequential redesign zone. It sits in the heart of a neighborhood that has absorbed hundreds of millions of dollars in new residential and mixed-use development over the past five years, much of it assembled with the BRT corridor woven into the investment thesis. [internal-link: Franklinton neighborhood coverage — tag page]

The redesign was triggered by traffic volume. ODOT's revised projections roughly doubled earlier traffic counts for this stretch. That matters because BRT stations and dedicated lanes in high-traffic corridors require more complex engineering: turning restrictions, signal priority systems, ADA-compliant platforms, and intersection redesigns. When the traffic model changes, the physical design often follows.

COTA says the dedicated guideway stays. That's meaningful. The BRT's real estate value proposition isn't just frequency — it's the permanence signal of fixed-infrastructure transit investment, the kind that walkable housing can command rent premiums around. If the bus lanes remain, the long-term investment thesis is intact, even if the timeline is not.

---

## What This Means for Developers and Investors

A 16-month delay from a projected 2026 construction start doesn't invalidate Franklinton or the Hilltop as investment corridors. But it changes the math in specific ways that operators need to address now.

**Hold periods extend.** Developers who underwrote a project assuming BRT service by 2027 may need to revisit exit assumptions or refinancing timelines. If service slips to 2029, the rent premium associated with BRT-adjacency also slips — and projects that need to stabilize before a refi face a longer window before that premium fully materializes.

**Financing conversations get harder.** Lenders who asked about the BRT corridor as a market tailwind will need updated answers. Savvy operators will get ahead of this: document COTA's continued commitment to the dedicated guideway, show the TOD study activity as proof of corridor momentum, and frame the delay as a timeline issue rather than a project risk.

**The corridor is still active.** COTA reported an 8.5% increase in transit service since November 2024 and has $29.7 million in bike, sidewalk, and trail infrastructure either in design or under construction along the LinkUS network in 2026. The corridor is not stalled — it is being upgraded. For developers, that distinction matters in lender conversations.

**TOD parcels near planned stations are still worth mapping.** COTA and the City of Columbus are continuing public meetings for the West Broad Corridor Transit-Oriented Development Study, focused on station area planning and affordable housing targets. Station area boundaries are where the first regulatory wins will come — zoning overlays, density bonuses, parking exemptions — regardless of when BRT service actually starts. Developers who stay close to that process are positioning early.

---

## The Longer View

The fundamentals driving Franklinton and the Hilltop remain intact: central location, low land cost relative to Short North and Italian Village, and a residential pipeline that is giving both neighborhoods meaningful density for the first time in a generation.

The BRT delay doesn't alter those fundamentals. What it alters is the timeline precision some operators used in their underwriting. The prudent move is to revisit pro formas with a conservative BRT assumption — service by 2028 or later rather than 2026–27 — and test whether the deal still pencils at that schedule.

If it does, the corridor holds. Columbus is not backing away from LinkUS. COTA has not signaled cancellation; it has signaled a design rework on one mile of a 9.3-mile corridor. That's a construction headache, not a program abandonment.

Franklinton operators who bought for the corridor's intrinsic characteristics — density-compatible zoning, proximity to Downtown, improving neighborhood fundamentals — are in a different position than operators who bought exclusively for the BRT catalyst. This delay matters most to the latter group. The former group should update their models and stay focused on execution.
