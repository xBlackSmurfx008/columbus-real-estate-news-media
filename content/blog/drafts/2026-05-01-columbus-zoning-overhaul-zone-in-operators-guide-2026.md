# Columbus Zoning Overhaul: What Operators Need to Know Now

Columbus is in the middle of rewriting rules it hasn't changed since the 1950s — and the specifics that will determine what you can build, where, and how tall are still being finalized right now.

For real estate operators in Columbus, this is not a background story. Zone In, the city's multi-phase zoning overhaul, has already rezoned nearly half the city. The detailed standards for what that rezoning actually permits at the block level are being written in 2026 — and there is a window to shape them before they lock.

Here is what you need to know.

---

## The Three-Year Overhaul, Briefly

Columbus has been rewriting its zoning code in phases since 2023. The code being replaced dated to the 1950s — written for a city that has since grown five times in size and more than doubled in population.

**Zone In Phase 1** (July 2024): Passed the first new code language in decades, establishing the legal framework for six new mixed-use zone designations along major corridors.

**Zone In Phase 2** (2025): Expanded the rezoning to cover 43% of the city's parcels — approximately 66,000 properties. Applied the new mixed-use zones to 12,300 parcels along Columbus's primary commercial corridors.

**The Columbus Growth Strategy** (adopted January 27, 2026): The city's first-ever citywide land use policy and map. This layer sits above the zoning code itself — it's the "why" that drives zone assignments. Its most significant effect: it overrides the patchwork of neighborhood-level land use maps that Columbus's 21 area commissions had created, some of which hadn't been updated since the 1990s.

The detailed Phase 2 building standards — specific heights allowed at each corridor type, how transitions between zones are handled, what parking provisions apply at which distance from transit — are being developed throughout 2026 with public input ongoing.

## What's Actually Different for Developers

**Parking minimums are gone in mixed-use zones.**

This is the most consequential change for urban-infill operators. The old code required a fixed number of parking spaces per residential unit regardless of transit access, site size, or neighborhood context. On smaller infill sites, that formula made projects financially unviable — you couldn't build the units the market wanted without also building a garage the project couldn't support.

Under Zone In, parking is developer-discretionary in the new mixed-use zones, with proximity to transit as the guiding factor. If you're within walking distance of a high-frequency transit stop, you decide how much parking your project carries.

Parking structures in Columbus cost $25,000 to $50,000 per space to build. On a 60-unit project, eliminating a 60-space parking requirement can cut $1.5M to $3M from the development cost — enough to materially change what rents or sale prices are needed to service debt.

**Mixed uses are by right.**

The old code strictly separated commercial and residential uses in most zones. Building a corner mixed-use building — ground-floor retail, apartments above — often required a variance or conditional use process, adding time and risk to a project.

In the six new mixed-use zones, this combination is permitted by right. No special hearing, no conditional approval.

**Heights are higher, and bonuses are available.**

Base building heights on mixed-use corridor sites are higher under Zone In than under the old code. Projects that include a defined percentage of below-market units can access bonus floors beyond the base allowance.

For developers on high-land-cost sites (downtown-adjacent, Short North edge, Italian Village), the bonus height can change what deals work without a public subsidy.

**Streamlined process.**

Previously, routine variances on urban infill sites could require multiple board hearings. Zone In consolidates the approval pathway for projects that fit the new permitted uses and heights.

## Where the Opportunity Is

Zone In Phase 2 covers 43% of the city, but the density and flexibility are concentrated along specific corridors. The highest-impact areas for mixed-use developers:

**High Street (Short North to Clintonville)** — The city's primary mixed-use spine. New height allowances are explicitly tied to BRT service frequency along this corridor. Sites that couldn't carry a 6-story building under the old code may now.

**West Broad Street (Franklinton through Hilltop)** — A BRT corridor (LinkUS, see below) where transit investment and mixed-use zoning are aligned. The Hilltop RISE affordable rehab project underway at 3085 W. Broad is an early signal of what the corridor can support. [internal link: Hilltop RISE story]

**Broad Street east** — Toward the east side, where density arguments are less established but land costs remain lower. Earlier-mover advantage for operators willing to work with longer lease-up timelines.

**Lane Avenue and Indianola** — The Clintonville / University District edge, where neighborhood opposition to density is real but the zoning now supports it.

**Downtown and Short North** — Already dense; Zone In primarily helps with infill sites and parking relief on tight parcels rather than unlocking new height.

[inline image: Zone In Columbus zoning map screenshot]

Industrial corridors — Alum Creek, the south-side freight precincts, the Rickenbacker submarket — are largely outside Zone In's mixed-use scope and governed by separate industrial zone provisions.

## The Political Tension Operators Should Track

The Columbus Growth Strategy's adoption was not unanimous. Two area commissions formally opposed it before City Council voted in January: the Greater South East Area Commission and the Clintonville Area Commission. Both argued that overriding neighborhood-level land use maps would reduce community control over how their neighborhoods change.

Downtown Columbus and North Linden area commissions supported adoption.

This tension matters practically. As the detailed Phase 2 building standards are developed in 2026, area commission objections may resurface at the parcel and corridor level — particularly in Clintonville, which has historically resisted higher-density development along Indianola and High Street.

Operators with sites in contested corridors should expect some projects to face neighborhood pushback even under the new code framework. Zone In changes what's permitted; it doesn't eliminate civic opposition.

## The Italian Village Signal: Out-of-Market Capital Is Reading the Map

The most immediate stress test of Zone In's new dynamics is playing out at 274 E. First Ave. in Italian Village, where Cincinnati-based developer Merus has proposed a five-building apartment complex on a 7-acre site currently occupied by the State Library of Ohio and the Ohioana Library.

The proposal calls for three 3-story buildings and two 4-story buildings, plus a parking garage — a total density that would have been far harder to justify or permit under the old code.

Merus's Director of Real Estate Development, Patrick Byrne, described the project as in the "very early, conceptual stage," with rezoning to mixed-use as a requirement. The Italian Village Commission reviewed the demolition and rezoning request on March 10, 2026. **[Outcome of March 10 hearing not confirmed at time of publication — needs source before publish.]**

Whether or not this specific project advances, the proposal signals something important: well-capitalized developers from outside Columbus are scanning the Zone In map for sites that are newly viable. Italian Village is a premium neighborhood. The fact that a Cincinnati firm is pursuing a 7-acre play there suggests the rezoning is being read far beyond Columbus's own development community.

## What Operators Should Do Right Now

**1. Check your zone assignment.**

Pull every site in your pipeline against the Zone In Columbus map at [zone-in-columbus.hub.arcgis.com](https://zone-in-columbus.hub.arcgis.com). The interactive map shows old and new zone designations side by side. Any site that moved into one of the six new mixed-use zones has a fundamentally different development envelope than it did under the previous code.

**2. Re-run your pro formas without parking minimums.**

For sites on mixed-use-zoned corridors, recalculate what the project supports without the parking structure the old code required. This alone may move deals from unviable to viable.

**3. Engage the standards process.**

The detailed Phase 2 building standards — the specific height at each corridor type, transition rules, district-level parking provisions — are still being written. The Zone In gallery at 141 N. Front St. in downtown Columbus is the primary public input venue. [Comment period through June 10 — verify current status at zone-in-columbus.hub.arcgis.com before relying on this date.] [needs source]

If a specific provision affects your pipeline, this is the moment to submit comment — not after the standards are adopted.

**4. Track the BRT corridors closely.**

LinkUS West Broad BRT and the Transit-Oriented Development planning process it has triggered (see below) are adding a second, compounding planning layer to the same corridors where Zone In is most active. West Broad in particular — Franklinton through Hilltop — is the intersection of mixed-use zoning, transit investment, and affordable housing pressure. [internal link: LinkUS BRT TOD story — coming soon]

**5. Understand the area commission landscape.**

Before committing to a site in a corridor with active area commission opposition (Clintonville, Greater South East), model in the cost of a contested approval process. Zone In has reduced the legal barriers; it has not eliminated neighborhood opposition.

---

## The Bottom Line

Columbus spent three years rewriting zoning rules that had been unchanged since the Eisenhower administration. The resulting framework — six new mixed-use zones, no parking minimums, bonus floors for affordable units, and mixed uses by right — is already in effect on 43% of the city's parcels.

The detailed standards that will determine what those zone designations actually permit at the block level are still being finalized. For Columbus operators, this is the moment to check your sites, re-run your numbers, and engage the process.

The next generation of Columbus real estate is being built inside this code. The window to shape it is open now.
