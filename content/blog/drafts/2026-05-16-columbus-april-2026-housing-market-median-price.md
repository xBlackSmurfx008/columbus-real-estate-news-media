# Columbus April 2026: Median Home Price Hits $346,500 as Sales Slow

*By CRE News Staff · May 16, 2026*

The Central Ohio housing market entered spring 2026 with a split personality: home values are rising at their fastest pace in two years, while the number of homes changing hands is falling sharply. The April 2026 data from the Columbus Realtors — covered by WOSU on May 14 — tells a bifurcated story that every broker, investor, operator, and buyer in the metro needs to understand before summer.

## The April Numbers

Columbus Realtors reported **2,202 closed sales** in April 2026, a **13.6% decline** from April 2025. Year-to-date closings are running 4.2% below last year's pace. At the same time, the **median sales price climbed 8.3% year-over-year to $346,500** — the highest April figure on record for the Columbus metro — while the average sales price rose 5.9% to $396,480.

Active inventory rose **13.4% to 5,027 homes**, giving buyers more choices than they've had since late 2022. Homes are sitting longer: average days on market stretched from 32 to **39 days**, a 22% increase that signals buyers have more time to act and negotiate than at any point during the post-pandemic run-up.

The county-level breakdowns are striking. **Franklin County** — the metro's densest housing market — saw closings fall 15.4% to 1,077 units, while the median price jumped 10.6% to $331,688. The price appreciation is steepest where the volume decline is deepest. **Delaware County**, Columbus's most affluent suburban ring, held relatively flat at 254 closings with a $539,000 median, signaling that the upper-tier market is absorbing rate pressure far better than the middle of the range.

## What's Driving the Bifurcation

The volume decline is not a demand problem — it's a rate lock-in and affordability problem working in tandem.

On the supply side, a homeowner who locked in a 3% mortgage in 2021 has little financial incentive to sell and re-enter the market at current rates. That dynamic — widely called the "golden handcuff" effect — has suppressed listing activity across Central Ohio for three years. Many would-be move-up sellers are sitting tight, which keeps resale inventory constrained even as buyer demand softens.

On the demand side, higher rates combined with a $346,500 median price mean that a buyer putting 10% down faces a monthly payment well above $2,400 before taxes and insurance — more than 40% above what the same purchase cost at 2020-era rates. That affordability math is pushing entry-level and first-time buyers to the margins, reducing the pool of eligible buyers at the lower end.

The result: fewer transactions, longer time on market, but still-elevated prices because the structural shortage of homes — particularly in attainable price ranges — hasn't resolved.

## The Inventory Build Is Real — But Context Matters

The 13.4% year-over-year inventory gain sounds substantial, but context matters. At 5,027 active listings, the Columbus metro remains well below what housing economists consider a balanced market (typically 5–7 months of supply). Current absorption rates suggest roughly **2.3 months of supply** — meaningfully healthier than the sub-1.2-month conditions of 2022, but still a seller's market by any conventional measure.

For buyers, the shift from 1.2 months to 2.3 months represents a real opening: more listings to compare, fewer bidding wars, and 39 days on market instead of 7 to conduct proper due diligence. Savvy buyers are using this window; less experienced ones are still calibrating to a market that moved fast for so long that patience feels foreign.

For sellers, the shift is equally real. Listings that were getting 12 offers in the first weekend are now sitting. Price expectations set by 2021–2022 comps are increasingly out of step with where transactions are clearing.

## What This Means by Segment

**Investors and buy-side operators:** The expanding inventory window is the first meaningful negotiating leverage signal since 2022. Franklin County's 15.4% volume drop — the steepest in the metro — tends to precede price moderation in lower price tiers and creates more off-market conversation opportunity. Value-add and rehab plays have more room than stabilized acquisitions at current cap rate spreads; the gap between purchase price appreciation and financing costs remains thin for anyone underwriting at market.

**Buy-and-hold portfolio operators:** The 8.3% median price appreciation updates portfolio valuations favorably, but the same math compresses cap rates on new acquisitions. Operators looking to grow need to either access distressed product — which remains rare in Columbus — or build in value-add assumptions from day one.

**Developers and builders:** Rising resale prices don't resolve the affordability gap. Most of the inventory building in the Columbus market is priced above $300,000 — the segment where demand already exists. The unmet demand is concentrated in the $175,000–$275,000 range, where new construction rarely pencils without public subsidy. Columbus's modular construction sector is beginning to address this gap — one local manufacturer is assembling apartment units indoors and targeting sub-market costs on projects like a 74-unit Maryland Avenue complex — but scale remains limited. **Brokers and agents:** Sellers who entered 2026 expecting first-weekend multiple offers are getting a reality check. The 39-day average DOM gives competent sellers' agents room to work — pricing discipline, staging, timing, and negotiation skill are genuinely differentiating again. For buyer's agents, the window to add value through diligence, creative structuring, and patient strategy has reopened.

**First-time and entry-level buyers:** More inventory and longer DOM create a real opportunity — the first in several years — for buyers who have been crowded out. The affordability math is still hard, but the competitive dynamics have shifted. Delaware County's price stability at $539,000 confirms that even the north suburbs aren't softening much; buyers seeking more leverage should focus on Franklin County's middle-tier submarkets and emerging corridors.

## Delaware County as Its Own Market

Delaware County's relative stability deserves its own note. With 254 closings (flat year-over-year) and a $539,000 median, the north suburbs are operating in an effectively separate market from Franklin County. Significant new residential construction is underway in Powell, Westerville, and Dublin, providing supply-side relief for a market that has historically run hot. Delaware County has decoupled from Franklin County's volume softening because the buyers in that price range have the income and down payment resources to absorb higher rates without exiting the market entirely.

## The Bigger Picture: Columbus in 2026

Columbus entered 2026 as one of the most cited Midwest investment markets in national surveys. The ULI/PwC Emerging Trends report projected 2.8–4.5% home price appreciation for the year — April's 8.3% jump already exceeds that single-year forecast on a year-over-year basis, though monthly data fluctuates.

The structural demand drivers remain in place. Intel's semiconductor campus in Licking County and Amazon's expanding logistics and data center footprint east of Columbus continue to drive relocation demand and household formation. New Albany and the I-270 corridor are absorbing new residents at a pace that feeds back into residential demand across the metro.

The question heading into summer is whether the inventory build continues. If more listings come to market — either because sellers accept that rates aren't going back to 3% soon, or because rate relief materializes and unlocks move-up demand — the Columbus market could see transaction volume recovery in Q3. If inventory plateaus, prices will continue to run. Watch the month-end Columbus Realtors data, and watch Franklin County's DOM trend in particular: when that number stops climbing, the window for buyers to press advantage narrows again.
